Make Your Sources Talk to Each Other

Critical integration and synthesis of scholarly sources

A citation at the end of a paragraph is not automatically integration. The rubric asks you to make sources build on each other and connect back to your own experience. This page walks you from the basic move all the way to a finished paragraph.

1Synthesis vs. summary stacking

Reporting your sources vs. connecting them

When you cite two sources, your reader is asking one silent question: why are these in the same paragraph? Synthesis answers it. "Summary stacking" (two summaries parked side by side) leaves the reader to figure it out. Here is the same source pair, done both ways.

✗ Summary stacking AVOID THIS

Bauer and Erdogan (2026) explain that emotionally intelligent leaders build stronger relationships because they understand how emotions influence communication and decision-making. Coronado-Maldonado and Benítez-Márquez (2023) concluded that empathy and emotional intelligence improve teamwork, collaboration, and leadership effectiveness.

What is missing: two true sentences, but nothing links them and no sentence is yours. You have reported. You have not connected.
✓ Synthesis AIM FOR THIS

Taken together, these sources tie emotional intelligence to the relational side of leadership. Bauer and Erdogan (2026) explain that emotionally intelligent leaders build stronger relationships, while Coronado-Maldonado and Benítez-Márquez (2023) extend this to the team level. Together, they suggest a leader's emotional awareness may be the basis for the trust teams run on.

What it adds: a shared frame up front, a hinge that shows how they relate, and a last sentence neither author wrote alone.

2Three moves that turn a stack into synthesis

The 3-move test: frame, hinge, your point

1

Open with the shared idea

Start with what the sources are about together, not with an author's name: "Taken together, these sources…"

2

Add a hinge that shows the link

The hinge tells the reader how the two ideas fit.

3

End with your own point

Delete both citations from your last sentence. If it still says something, that is your thinking, and that is synthesis.

✗ Both authors studied emotional intelligence. Delete the citations and nothing is left.

✓ Emotional awareness may be what turns individual skill into team trust. It still stands on its own.

3Handle "similarly" and "likewise" with care

The word alone is not synthesis

These words are not banned; they are overused. Writers reach for them to sound connected without doing the connecting. If you use one, it must be true (the sources really do point the same way), and you still owe the reader your own point afterward.

✗ The trap

Bauer and Erdogan (2026) found delegation builds followers' confidence. Similarly, Samman (2026) found empowerment increases ownership.

The word promises a link it never delivers. Still a stack.
✓ Earned

Bauer and Erdogan (2026) found delegation builds followers' confidence. Similarly, Samman (2026) tied empowerment to ownership, suggesting the confidence one describes may drive the ownership the other observed.

4Cite it, then synthesize it

Claim, cite, hinge, your wrap-up

A strong claim is not yours to keep until you show where it came from. Here is the whole build: make the claim, cite each part, then (if the sources allow) synthesize them into a point that is yours.

  1. 1

    Start with the claim

    Leaders can balance performance incentives with ethical and relational motivations by broadening how success is defined, but financial rewards may ignore unequal access to opportunity.

  2. 2

    Cite each part, because this is not common knowledge

    Leaders can balance performance incentives with ethical and relational motivations by broadening how success is defined and recognized (Zhang & Liu, 2022). Rowland and Hall (2012) found that while financial rewards can boost immediate performance, they may fail to account for unequal access to opportunities.
  3. 3

    Synthesize with a hinge word and your wrap-up

    …broadening how success is defined and recognized (Zhang & Liu, 2022); however, Rowland and Hall (2012) found that financial rewards may fail to account for unequal access to opportunities. Taken together, these sources explain that by pairing incentive systems with fair, relationship-centered recognition and equitable access, leaders can drive strong results without compromising ethics or inclusion.

⚡ Not every source can be synthesized

  1. You need at least two scholarly sources.
  2. Do not force a relationship that is not there. Sources must complement, build on, or contradict each other.

5When it is your own experience

Your story sets it up; the sources explain it

Sometimes you are asked to draw on your own experience, and that is allowed. But the rubric still wants scholarly sources woven in. The move: tell what you saw, then bring in sources that explain the phenomenon you witnessed.

Experience alone NOT ENOUGH YET

In my past, I have seen leaders balance performance incentives with ethical and relational motivations by broadening how success is defined. I have also seen financial rewards boost immediate performance, but the gains fade after a short time.

What is missing: it is honest and observed, but there is no scholarship explaining why those reward programs faded. The rubric still needs critical integration of sources.
✓ Experience + sources NOW IT COUNTS

…the gains fade after a short time. Both programs failed to include intrinsic motivation, and I now understand why. Deci et al. (1999) provide evidence that relying heavily on performance-contingent rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, weakening long-term engagement even when short-term performance improves. Building on that, Adams (2025) argues organizations should intentionally balance extrinsic incentives with intrinsic rewards: purpose, autonomy, growth. Taken together, these sources suggest reward programs built solely on extrinsic incentives risk undermining the internal drivers that sustain performance, so effective programs should recognize success in ways that reinforce both measurable outcomes and intrinsic commitment.

6Putting it together: before and after

Same student, same idea, very different score

Here is one student paragraph before and after revision. Watch how every move from this page shows up in the finished version.

Exhibit A: Before ONE SOURCE, BOLTED ON

This experience brought the importance of leadership to light, not just in accomplishing organizational tasks, but also in knowing how to respond when employees are emotionally moved, how to react to it, and how to make them feel appreciated and valued. Empowering employees to express themselves, do meaningful work, and grow improves employee motivation and organizational commitment (Cable, 2018).

What is happening: the personal claims run unsupported, then a single citation is dropped at the very end like a footnote apology. The source sits beside the argument rather than advancing it.
Exhibit B: After THREE SOURCES IN CONVERSATION

Staying calm while showing empathy toward frustrated, stressed workers proved essential. This aligns with Goleman's (2004) argument that self-regulation (managing one's own emotional reactions) enables leaders to respond to others rather than merely react. Rather than reacting emotionally, I learned to listen carefully, communicate openly, and collaborate to minimize misunderstandings. These behaviors reflect the relational core of servant leadership, which Eva et al. (2019) describe as prioritizing followers' needs and psychological well-being over the leader's own agenda. Crucially, this emotional attentiveness is not separate from organizational performance but a driver of it: when employees feel empowered to express themselves, do meaningful work, and grow, both their motivation and organizational commitment increase (Cable, 2018). Taken together, leadership is not just accomplishing tasks; it is the practice of making people feel valued so that the work itself becomes sustainable.

What changed

  1. Sources talk to each other. Goleman explains the internal skill (self-regulation), Eva et al. name the philosophy it serves (servant leadership), and Cable supplies the organizational payoff. Each citation earns its place by advancing the argument.
  2. Your experience is the connective tissue. The paragraph leads with a personal claim, then cites a source to explain why it is true. Experience raises a question; scholarship answers it.
  3. Transition words do structural work. "Crucially," "notably," "then," and "not separate from… but" stitch the sources together. Quick test: if you can delete every citation and the paragraph still flows perfectly, the sources are decoration, not integration.

7Before you cite: AI and your sources

Read it, verify it, then use it

⚠ One honest warning about AI and sources

AI is a fine place to start finding sources, but you must read and verify each one, or you cannot use it. And do not write your paragraph first, then ask AI to "find sources that agree." That flips the academic process backward: sources are meant to inform and challenge your thinking, not rubber-stamp it.

The one exception: finding sources to explain a phenomenon you personally witnessed, which is exactly what When it is your own experience is about.

A note on the example sources

Goleman (2004) and Eva et al. (2019) appear in Exhibit B as illustrative examples to show what synthesis looks like. Both are real, well-known works on emotional intelligence and servant leadership, but verify the exact year, page, and claim against the actual articles before treating any wording as a citable fact.

Never cite a source you have not read.
